The Problem

Shared air / unequal choices

Almaty’s smog is an unequal exposure to a shared atmosphere. This project focuses on outdoor fine particulate matter, PM₂.₅, across the city and its connected suburban airshed, especially during the heating season. It asks what a small public AI system could contribute when the difficult decisions concern energy, money and accountability.

The Bureau of National Statistics records 2,392,100 city residents on 1 October 2026. That administrative count excludes additional suburban commuters. The scale is therefore metropolitan, while this proposal tests a deliberately limited neighbourhood service rather than promising immediate citywide coverage. [30]

IQAir’s historical city table reports annual PM₂.₅ concentrations of 20.3 µg/m³ in 2024 and 22.8 µg/m³ in 2025. The latter is 4.56 times the WHO annual guideline of 5 µg/m³, rounded here to 4.6. These are provider aggregates, not a personal exposure measurement or a complete census of neighbourhood air. The WHO guideline is a health benchmark, not Kazakhstan’s legal limit or a boundary below which risk disappears. [1][2]

The problem predates these recent figures. Kerimray and colleagues’ analysis of 2013–2018 observations found pronounced winter peaks and lower particulate concentrations at higher elevations. A subsequent year-long chemical sampling campaign, August 2022–July 2023, identified multiple sources in Almaty and Astana, with coal and biomass combustion important across the source mixture. This establishes persistence across research periods, without pretending that different networks form one continuous trend. [5][8]

The World Bank’s 2022 city assessment links population exposure to heating and power generation as well as transport. Its model uses a 2018 baseline, so its source shares cannot simply be relabelled “today’s emissions.” Geography helps pollution accumulate; institutions decide which fuels remain affordable, which sources are controlled, and how quickly infrastructure changes. [3]

Exposure and the ability to avoid it differ. Children at school, older residents, people with existing illness and outdoor workers face different vulnerabilities and constraints. A resident who can buy filtration and work indoors has options unavailable to a delivery worker or a household struggling with heating costs. These are the project’s distributional concerns; the available city averages do not quantify each group’s relative exposure. [2]

PM₂.₅ means particles with an aerodynamic diameter of 2.5 micrometres or less. Their small size makes them a health concern even when pollution is not visibly dramatic. This project keeps PM₂.₅ separate from nitrogen dioxide and from an air-quality index: a change in one pollutant or index does not automatically describe the others. [2]

The social-science question

Can publicly accountable monitoring and forecasting make Almaty’s clean-air decisions more responsive to heavily exposed communities, without shifting responsibility from major emitters and government onto residents who have the fewest choices?

The unit of intervention is therefore a neighbourhood institution and its public decision process, rather than an individual “smart” consumer. Success means that evidence changes a funded response and that underserved areas can contest its distribution. A more accurate map is useful, but cleaner air requires changes in emissions and protection, not simply a better description of the same harm.

Background & Data

What is measured / what is missing

There is no single number called “Almaty air pollution.” An inventory measures emissions, a monitor measures concentrations at one location, and an exposure model combines pollution with where people live. Treating them as interchangeable can make a persuasive chart analytically wrong.

The Bureau of National Statistics publishes stationary-source emissions and environmental expenditure. These data describe reported quantities released and spending, rather than street-level concentrations. Kazhydromet, under the ecology ministry, measures ambient pollutants and reports monitoring locations and exceedances. A concentration observation does not by itself identify an emitter. [7][4]

WHO and the World Bank supply health benchmarks, exposure modelling and policy scenarios, not current readings. IQAir and researchers contribute provider aggregates and targeted measurements; neither automatically supplies a population-representative network. Combining these sources requires retaining the distinction between what is emitted, what is measured in air, and what people actually inhale. [2][3][1][8]

Kazhydromet’s July 2025 bulletin describes 16 observation posts: four manual and twelve automatic. Their pollutant coverage differs; sixteen posts does not mean sixteen comparable PM₂.₅ instruments. Ministry-linked administrative and emissions records help identify regulated sources, while household energy data are needed to understand dispersed combustion. International assessments organise these inputs; they do not remove their limitations. [4][7]

A second comparison shows why interpretation matters. During the 2020 lockdown, mean PM₂.₅ was lower than on the same dates in the preceding years. Yet February-to-April reductions also occurred without lockdown. The paired bars below reproduce the published table, making the seasonal comparison visible instead of attributing every improvement to reduced traffic. [6]

Where the numbers go silent

Coverage is not random. Instruments need secure locations, electricity and maintenance. Neighbourhoods without reliable sensors, suburban commuters and people moving between home, work and school are poorly represented by a city mean. Indoor infiltration and working conditions are missing. A household cannot infer its child’s dose from a district-coloured map.

Measurement is conditional. Optical sensors respond to humidity, aerosol composition and ageing. Calibration learned in one climate may fail in Almaty’s winter. The World Bank documented implausible readings and missing observations in the official data it assessed. Missing periods must remain visible, rather than being silently replaced by confident-looking predictions. [12][3]

Comparability and attribution are separate tests. A monitoring network can change between years; weather can change even when instruments do not. Chemical source apportionment also answers a different question from an inventory of tonnes emitted. Trust requires station histories, calibration records, data-completeness rules and uncertainty. This project therefore reports dates and measurement scope, preserves published values, and makes no claim that the two annual provider averages prove a worsening long-term trajectory.

Silence also concerns outcomes. A delivered alert is not evidence that a worker could change shifts or that a school had usable filtration. Evaluation needs interviews and institutional response records alongside sensor data. Publish district-level availability and missingness before comparing districts; an apparently cleaner area may simply have fewer valid observations. Health records, if ever studied, would require a separate ethics and access process rather than becoming a default input.

3

Current State of Research

Evidence organised by approach

The research falls into four connected approaches: explaining the pollution mixture, estimating health burdens, predicting concentrations, and examining public authority. They offer different kinds of evidence. A source model cannot demonstrate public consent; a reliable forecast cannot establish a health benefit.

01 / Sources, seasons and natural experiments

Almaty’s observational literature establishes spatial and seasonal structure. Kerimray et al.’s spatiotemporal study associates particulate pollution with winter and elevation. Its strength is making urban variation visible; its limitation is that correlation with cold weather or proximity does not uniquely identify an emitter. Heating demand and atmospheric dispersion change together. The authors’ interpretation supports investigating combustion, without converting each correlation into a causal source share. [5]

Tursun et al. move from concentrations towards composition, using positive matrix factorisation of chemically characterised particles from a year-long campaign. Their findings identify a mixed source profile and important combustion influences. This approach narrows plausible origins, but source labels depend on the chemical markers, sampling design and model interpretation. A year of samples is stronger than a dramatic smog-day photograph; it still does not represent every street, year or household. [8]

The lockdown study provides a useful interruption to ordinary activity. It reports a 21% reduction in PM₂.₅ relative to the same dates in 2018–2019, while warning that meteorology complicates attribution. It also uses different comparison periods for different pollutants. Figure 2 illustrates the resulting lesson: traffic matters, but a simple before-and-after comparison cannot isolate its contribution, and less traffic did not eliminate particulate pollution. [6]

Regionally, Guttikunda et al. combine emissions inventories and WRF–CAMx modelling for Bishkek. Heating, transport, dust and waste are assessed together, and cleaner-fuel scenarios imply substantial potential improvements. These are modelled possibilities rather than completed interventions. The regional comparison supports an airshed perspective, while differences in fuels, topography and institutions prevent importing Bishkek’s source percentages directly into Almaty. [9]

Source attribution remains contested partly because studies estimate different quantities. A model of population-weighted exposure and a chemical sample at selected sites need not return identical shares. Their disagreement should motivate triangulation and updated inventories, not a contest for one permanently correct percentage. The World Bank’s regional synthesis likewise connects cleaner air with heating reform and management capacity, reinforcing the limits of a forecast-only intervention. [3][24]

02 / Health burdens and uncertain translation

Global epidemiology explains why sustained PM₂.₅ exposure matters beyond visible discomfort. Burnett et al.’s Global Exposure Mortality Model draws on 41 cohorts from 16 countries. Its comparison with earlier risk functions demonstrates that estimated mortality burdens depend materially on model choice. This evidence supports reducing exposure; it does not justify calculating an exact number of Almaty lives “saved by AI” without a measured exposure change, population structure and a defensible local baseline. [10]

The unresolved distributional question is whose exposure actually falls. A city mean can improve while an underserved area remains heavily exposed. For this project, average concentration, monitoring coverage and access to protective action must be assessed separately. Otherwise an intervention could look successful in aggregate while leaving its intended beneficiaries behind. Qualitative interviews would examine heating affordability, trust in warnings and barriers to action; these are questions for fieldwork, not findings invented for this proposal.

03 / Machine learning, measurement and prediction

Grange et al. use random forests to normalise Swiss PM₁₀ trends for meteorology. Their approach helps distinguish weather variation from changes more consistent with emissions or chemistry. It makes a useful methodological starting point for evaluation, but Swiss PM₁₀ performance does not validate an Almaty PM₂.₅ forecast. Nor does statistical normalisation eliminate every unmeasured confounder. [11]

Adong et al.’s AirQo study shows that machine learning can improve low-cost sensor calibration in an operational network. It also exposes the dependence of attractive maps on reference instruments and fieldwork. Transfer to Almaty would require local co-location, winter validation and tests on unseen stations. Complex models must outperform simpler alternatives; “AI” is not itself a quality standard. [12]

04 / Institutions, legitimacy and relational ethics

Buribayeva et al. identify risks embedded in Kazakhstan’s public-sector AI institutions, including concentrated infrastructure, opacity and fragmented responsibility. Their legal analysis and secondary analysis of 53 interviews map potential mechanisms, rather than proving harms from an air-quality system. Roberts et al. emphasise coordination and capacity within existing governance institutions. Together these approaches favour identifiable public duties over another technology platform with unclear ownership. [14][15]

Mhlambi’s Ubuntu framework adds a relational test: whether technology strengthens community dignity and participation, rather than merely processing individuals efficiently. Applied here, this means residents help choose monitoring priorities and response rules. This is a normative design commitment, not empirical proof that participation alone reduces pollution. [16]

The gap this proposal addresses

The reviewed evidence supports better measurement and cautious forecasting. It does not establish that an Almaty neighbourhood AI service would improve equitable public responses. The pilot tests that missing link: does locally validated information trigger timely, accessible action, with benefits and errors reported by neighbourhood?

4

The AI-Assisted Solution,
and What It Would Cost

Common Air Almaty / a conditional proposal

The proposal is Common Air Almaty: a twelve-month, twenty-sensor pilot connecting locally calibrated measurements and next-day forecasts to a public response protocol. The information service supports clean-air policy; its budget does not purchase a power-plant conversion or eliminate household fuel poverty.

A small system with a named public owner

The city’s environmental administration would commission the pilot with Kazhydromet and a university evaluation team. A competitively selected contractor maintains equipment and software. A resident panel, including school communities and outdoor workers, reviews coverage and communications. These are proposed roles, not existing partnerships. Siting would prioritise coverage gaps, contrasting elevations and heating environments, rather than only convenient central locations.

Organise twenty nodes into four neighbourhood clusters, with five per cluster and school communities represented. Publish selection criteria and rejected locations. Installations depend on host permission, safe power and reference access; administrative convenience must not determine who becomes visible.

The system combines PM₂.₅, humidity, temperature and station metadata with licensed meteorological inputs. Random forests calibrate sensors against co-located reference measurements; gradient-boosted trees test next-day district forecasts. Both are compared with linear calibration and a persistence forecast, which predicts that tomorrow resembles today. Publish prediction intervals, missing-data flags and errors by location. The public interface provides Kazakh and Russian bulletins, school notices and optional district SMS alerts.

A forecast only becomes useful through a response. Schools receive an agreed activity and indoor-air protocol; the city publishes who reviews pollution episodes and whether inspections or heating-support referrals follow. Existing authorised agencies retain decisions. A model cannot identify a guilty household from a high reading. Any new filtration or heating assistance needs its own approved funding.

Case 01 / Kampala, Uganda

AirQo: useful AI begins with calibration

Built: machine-learning correction for low-cost particulate sensors, validated against reference monitors. Achieved: random-forest correction reduced PM₂.₅ root-mean-square error from 18.6 to 7.2 µg/m³ in the study; calibration models were deployed across more than 120 devices. These results support practical monitoring, not a claim of reduced disease. Almaty needs its own winter calibration. [12]

Case 02 / Pittsburgh, United States

SURTRAC: connect information to action

Built: AI planning that coordinated signals at nine intersections. Achieved: the field study reports 25.79% shorter travel times and 40.64% less waiting; its 21.48% emissions reduction is modelled, not a measured PM₂.₅ change. The U.S. transport department summarises the same study. [13][25]

The developer-led comparison used March and June 2012 runs, limiting causal certainty. Its lesson is an operational response to information. Almaty should not import the effect size: traffic optimisation cannot replace heating reform, and this pilot does not purchase signal control.

What would count as success?

Pre-register evaluation before procurement. Hold out entire stations and winter periods, preventing training–test leakage. Report calibration error, forecast error, missed severe episodes and false alarms against baselines. A proposed continuation gate is at least 10% lower held-out mean absolute forecast error than persistence, without poorer performance in priority districts. This is a decision threshold, not a promised result. Track delivery and agency response times, including non-digital reach. One year cannot establish a mortality effect; emissions claims need a separate comparison design.

Months one and two establish permissions, reference co-location and a data-quality audit; the service then operates through month eleven, with an independent decision in month twelve. Historical training data must pass that audit. If reliable winter examples are insufficient, remain in monitoring and shadow-forecast mode until prospective validation is possible. Publish the delay and revised cost rather than claiming readiness. Compare a simple bulletin with AI-supported bulletins during evaluation, while giving every participating community the same basic public-health information.

Assess the continuation threshold with uncertainty, not one favourable average. An improvement that disappears at unseen stations is insufficient. Record why agencies acted or declined to act, and let residents report inaccessible advice. These observations test whether the institutional response, rather than merely the prediction score, improved.

Economic / Who pays, who benefits, who profits?

The city would fund the service from its environmental budget, with public procurement and a fixed contractor fee. Suppliers and paid researchers earn revenue; residents benefit only if better information produces accessible protection or emissions action. Residents should not pay subscriptions. Cost is an opportunity cost: compare this pilot with reference monitoring, manual bulletins and direct mitigation. Obtain local quotations, employment on-costs, data licences and reference-instrument access before approval; the contingency is not proof those costs are sufficient.

A published market reference is AirGradient’s assembled Open Air at US$225. At an illustrative, not quoted, exchange rate of ₸500/US$, that is ₸112,500 before delivery. The ₸250,000 package allowance leaves room for imports, spares and winter adaptation, subject to quotations and cold-weather testing; it is not a product endorsement. BNS reports a 2025 national median monthly wage of ₸317,512. The table’s higher technician and analyst rates are assumed charged monthly equivalents requiring an employer-cost breakdown, not observed job-specific salaries. [26][27]

Environmental / Count electricity and water

Assume twenty sensors drawing 5 W continuously: 20 × 0.005 × 8,760 = 876 kWh/year. Allocate 100 W to a shared server and 200 W for 100 training hours. Apply PUE 1.56, Uptime Institute’s 2024 survey benchmark, to computing electricity: (876 + 20) × 1.56 = 1,397.76 kWh. Including sensors gives 2,273.76 kWh, approximately 2.27 MWh/year. PUE includes facility overhead; local performance and any winter heating must be metered. [28]

Ember reports that coal supplied 55% of Kazakhstan’s electricity in 2024. A local server therefore cannot be assumed carbon-free. A deliberately hypothetical emissions-factor range of 0.5–1.0 kg CO₂e/kWh gives 1.14–2.27 tonnes annually; this is sensitivity analysis, not an official grid-factor estimate. [23]

Server and training IT energy totals 896 kWh. Assuming direct cooling-water use of 0.2–2 litres per IT kWh implies approximately 0.18–1.79 m³/year. For comparison, Microsoft reports global FY24 WUE of 0.30 L/kWh; this does not establish Kazakhstan’s intensity. The boundary excludes electricity-generation water, manufacturing and fieldwork. Require local disclosures, repairability and responsible disposal; efficient software does not erase hardware’s embodied footprint. [29]

Labour / The work behind the dashboard

Technicians clean, co-locate and replace sensors; an analyst labels faults and reviews model drift; the coordinator checks translations and handles complaints. The labour allowances are not estimates of salaries already paid locally. Work shifts from manual reporting towards validation and maintenance, so no staff reductions are assumed. Preserve professional judgement, paid training and safe winter fieldwork. Unpaid students or residents must not become the hidden maintenance workforce.

Retain disputed fault labels and review reasons. Otherwise rushed annotation can erase genuine pollution episodes as supposed sensor errors. Contracted maintenance time and a documented escalation route protect both workers and the evidence.

Political and governance / Who can challenge the system?

Kazakhstan’s Law No. 230-VIII establishes risk-based AI governance. Determine the applicable classification and current implementing duties before deployment; “environmental” does not automatically mean low risk. Publish the purpose, accountable agency, model documentation, procurement contract and appeal route. Keep decisions with named officials. Ban integration with facial recognition, police tracking or individual household scoring. These are enforceable procurement conditions proposed here, not claims that the law already mandates every safeguard. [17]

Buribayeva et al.’s warning about fragmented responsibility makes an independent complaint reviewer essential. Roberts et al.’s coordination argument supports agreements between existing agencies instead of competing platforms. The AI Development Concept 2024–2029 supplies national policy context; alignment with a strategy cannot substitute for evidence of public benefit. [14][15][22]

The EU AI Act provides comparative benchmarks for documentation, risk management and human oversight; its high-risk requirements do not automatically govern this Kazakh pilot. The OECD Principles translate into traceable decisions, understandable limitations, a challenge process and a safe shutdown procedure. These commitments need an owner and a budget, rather than a decorative ethics label. [19][20]

UNESCO’s Recommendation requires an ethical perspective on participation, proportionality and environmental impacts. The pilot would publish an impact assessment before installation. Mhlambi’s Ubuntu framework supports a resident panel with influence over siting and responses, including people unable to buy private protection. Participation must affect decisions, not merely endorse a finished technical design. [21][16]

Data ethics / Measure air without profiling people

Environmental readings need timestamps and sensor locations, not names, medical files or personal travel histories. Obtain host permission for installations and legal access to data feeds. Optional SMS enrolment requires a clear consent and withdrawal process; store phone numbers separately and delete them promptly after withdrawal. Kazakhstan’s personal-data law requires domestic storage of personal data under Article 12(2). Environmental data are not automatically personal data. [18]

Publish district aggregates and quality flags; restrict contact lists and sensitive host details. Audit whether sensor gaps and translation errors disadvantage particular communities. False reassurance, alert fatigue and stigmatised neighbourhoods are foreseeable harms. A failed sensor must display “data unavailable”; it must never generate a reassuring green label by default.

The verdict

Yes to a bounded pilot.
Expansion must be earned.

Proceed only with reference access, a funded response protocol, public ownership of usable data, paid maintenance and independent evaluation. Scale only if the system improves on simpler baselines and reaches priority communities. Publish failure as well as success. If city leaders will fund a dashboard but not the responses it recommends, the answer is no. Almaty needs cleaner heating and enforceable public responsibility; AI earns a role only by helping deliver them.
